Abstract Human activities profoundly impact both
biotic and abiotic components of ecosystems. Pol-
lution, defined as the introduction of harmful sub-
stances into the environment, poses a significant
threat to all living organisms. Even minimal concen-
trations of pollutants—whether in gaseous, liquid, or
solid form—can disrupt ecosystem health. The advent
of the Industrial Revolution marked the beginning
of large-scale pollutant emissions, leading to severe
repercussions for both human health and the envi-
ronment. While the Industrial Revolution facilitated
numerous advancements in services, science, and
societal progress, it also introduced substantial envi-
ronmental challenges. Today, the unprecedented lev-
els of industrialization and urbanization have ampli-
fied global environmental concerns. Human-induced
air pollution alone accounts for approximately 9
million deaths annually, presenting a critical public
health threat. This research examines the environ-
mental contamination arising from human activities,
with a focus on water quality, air quality, and climate
change. It aims to provide a comprehensive under-
standing of the environmental impacts of human
actions, highlighting the pressing global issue of
water pollution, particularly in developing countries.

1	Introduction
The "environment" refers to the complex and diverse
settings in which a vast array of species, such as
humans, animals, bacteria, and plants, live and inter-
act. This environment is composed of several funda-
mental elements, each of which is indispensable for
the sustenance of life and the preservation of eco-
logical balance. These elements include land, which
provides a foundation and resources for terrestrial
life; water, which is essential for all forms of life and
plays a key role in various biological and chemical
processes; and the atmosphere, which offers the air
we breathe and helps regulate the Earth's climate.
Together, these components create a dynamic and
interconnected system that supports the diverse forms
of life on our planet and maintains the natural equilib-
rium necessary for their survival and well-being.
Pollutants, however, are defined as substances
that are introduced into the environment in quantities
exceeding their natural levels. These excess pollutants
can disrupt the delicate balance of ecosystems and
lead to detrimental effects on both the environment

and the species that depend on it Briffa et al., 2020.
The significant influence of human activities on the
environment has led to extensive research focusing on
the dynamic interactions between humans and their
physical surroundings. These studies aim to under-
stand how human actions affects the environment and
how these impacts can be managed or mitigated to
ensure sustainable development Peng & Jia, 2023
The environment is composed of both biotic and
abiotic components. Biotic components include all
living organisms, from the smallest microorgan-
isms to the largest animals and plants. These living
beings interact with each other and their surround-
ings in intricate ways. Abiotic components, on the
other hand, consist of non-living elements such as the
atmosphere, lithosphere (the earth's crust and upper
mantle), and hydrosphere (all the water bodies on
earth). These non-living elements provide the essen-
tial resources and conditions needed for life to thrive
Huggett, 2023.
Understanding the interrelationship between biotic
and abiotic components is crucial for environmen-
tal conservation and management. The interactions
between living organisms and their non-living envi-
ronment form the basis of ecosystems, which are vital
for maintaining the earth's biodiversity and the over-
all health of the planet. Despite their harmful effects,
human activities, contaminating water, air, and the
environment at large Tumolo et al., 2020 produce
toxic substances in solid, liquid, or gaseous forms.
Over the past century, rapid industrial growth has
improved living standards by offering a variety of ser-
vices. However, this development has also led to the
excessive exploitation of natural resources, increasing
pollution levels. This phenomenon has been widely
acknowledged as having a great deal of utility, but
it also has significant drawbacks Crane-Kramer &
Buckberry, 2023.
Pollution introduces a wide array of pollutants such
as inorganic, organic, and organometallic chemicals,
gases, nanoparticles, viruses, and radioactive isotopes
into the environment, posing a recognized environ-
mental hazard. These contaminants, discharged into
the atmosphere, water bodies, and the overall envi-
ronment, present a substantial risk to human health
Aliyu & Amadu, 2017. In developing nations striving
for economic advancement, water pollution emerges
as a significant issue stemming from diverse sources
such as industrial discharges, inadequate waste
management, agricultural runoff, oil spills, sewage,
and wastewater Lin et al., 2022. This pollution leads
to diminished oxygen levels, biodiversity loss, habi-
tat destruction, and disruptions in aquatic ecosystems,
profoundly affecting fish and other aquatic organisms.
Consequently, aquatic populations suffer, and ecosys-
tems face degradation risks.
Contaminated water also spreads waterborne dis-
eases such as cholera, hepatitis, and dysentery, and
has long-term effects on growth, development, repro-
duction, and cancer rates Alao et al., 2023. Addition-
ally, economic losses from reduced tourism revenue
and water cleanup costs result from pollution. To
achieve sustainable development, strategies to pre-
vent water pollution and effectively treat wastewater
before disposal must be implemented Ayejoto et al.,
2023. Given the constant movement of air, it can eas-
ily become contaminated and transfer pollutants from
one place to another.
In our pursuit of sustainable development, it is
imperative to implement targeted solutions that effec-
tively reduce water pollution. Central to this effort
is the deployment of efficient wastewater treatment
systems, crucial for safeguarding our water resources
from contamination Silva, 2023. Historical research
from the 1980s uncovered a startling revelation: sul-
phur dioxide emissions, stemming from coal com-
bustion in the Ohio Valley, travelled unprecedented
distances via wind currents. This phenomenon led
to the formation of acid rain, which had far-reaching
consequences, affecting extensive regions of the east-
ern United States and Canada, stretching thousands of
kilometres beyond its origin in the Ohio Valley Gren-
nfelt et al., 2020. A comparable scenario unfolds in
Asia, where powerful winds carry industrial pollut-
ants from China across the vast expanse of the Gobi
Desert. These pollutants accumulate during their
journey, resulting in the formation of substantial yel-
low dust storms that adversely affect regions such as
Japan and the Korean Peninsula. These storms not
only diminish visibility but also pose significant risks
to human health Agbasi et al., 2023.
Furthermore, stagnant air conditions created by
high-pressure systems exacerbate air pollution. This
stagnation allows pollutants such as emissions from
factories and vehicle exhaust to concentrate at hazard-
ous levels, increasing the likelihood of carbon monox-
ide poisoning Hayasaka et al., 2019. The "greenhouse
effect" refers to the natural process by which certain

3	Comprehensive assessment on water pollution
The introduction of toxic compounds into surface
water (such as lakes, rivers, and oceans) or subsur-
face water (such as aquifers) is known as water pol-
lution. These contaminants disrupt natural ecosystem
processes or diminish the availability of clean water
for beneficial uses Poikane et al., 2020. Energy emis-
sions, including heat and radiation, can also contrib-
ute to water pollution Sathya et al., 2022, alongside
the discharge of chemicals, waste, or pathogens into
water bodies. Human health suffers significantly from
exposure to unsafe water. Approximately 300,000
children under five years old die annually due to diar-
rhoea accounting for about 5.3% of total deaths Sol-
omon et al., 2021. Additionally, studies in Palestine
indicate a higher incidence of diarrhoea among those
who consume untreated municipal water compared
to those using desalinated or home-filtered water
Mustafa et al., 2023.
Water is indispensable for a multitude of industrial,
environmental, and physiological processes. It plays
vital roles such as acting as a solvent, lubricant, tem-
perature regulator, metabolite, and providing a habitat
for countless organisms Arora et al., 2021. Despite
its essential functions, water quality is often compro-
mised when pollutants from various human activities
exceed acceptable standards, making it unsuitable for
its intended uses Ewuzie et al., 2021. This contami-
nation is particularly concerning given water's crucial
importance to all living organisms, including humans.
Polluted water poses severe threats to environmen-
tal integrity, disrupting ecosystems and endangering
human health. The specific impacts of these pollut-
ants can vary widely depending on their nature and
source Silva, 2023.
Moreover, the issue of water contamination is
exacerbated by the looming global water scarcity
crisis. Projections indicate that by 2025, five billion
people out of an estimated global population of eight
billion will be living in regions facing water scarcity
Liu et al., 2024. This alarming forecast underscores
the urgent need for sustainable water management
practices to ensure that water remains available and
safe for all its essential uses, thereby protecting both
ecological balance and human health. The release of
industrial and chemical substances poses one of the
most significant environmental challenges humanities
faces today. Although many of these pollutants are
present in trace amounts, their potential for causing
significant toxicological effects cannot be overlooked.
This risk is amplified when these pollutants inter-
act in complex combinations, creating a cocktail of
harmful substances.
As noted by Schwarzenbach et al., 2006, the
presence of these pollutants in our water systems is
widespread and growing. Table 1 provides a com-
prehensive overview of common water pollutants
and their associated impacts. It includes pharmaceu-
ticals, which can disrupt aquatic ecosystems even at
low concentrations; industrial chemicals, which often
persist in the environment and accumulate in the food
chain; pesticides, which can be toxic to a wide range
of organisms; steroid hormones, which can interfere
with the reproductive systems of aquatic life; and
other emerging substances that are increasingly being
detected in water sources.
The global significance of freshwater contamina-
tion as a serious public concern should not be under-
estimated Narwal et al., 2023. For a comprehensive
understanding of the various challenges related to
sewage and waste management resulting from human
activities, it is recommended to explore extensive
resources on waste disposal and solid waste manage-
ment. These resources provide detailed information
on the methods and practices used to address and mit-
igate the impact of waste on the environment, includ-
ing the collection, treatment, and disposal of sewage,
as well as the management of solid waste materials
Englande et al., 2015. Water pollution can arise from
point sources or nonpoint sources. Point sources, like
discharge pipes from municipal or industrial sewage
systems, localize pollutants to specific areas, simpli-
fying management in comparison to diffuse sources
Babcock-Jackson et al., 2023. Nonpoint sources, like
runoff from agricultural fields, pose a broader chal-
lenge as pollutants enter water systems over expan-
sive and unconfined areas.
While advancements in sewage treatment technol-
ogies have improved management of diffuse pollution
sources, significant challenges remain Fu et al., 2024.

3.1	Causes of Water Contamination
3.1.1	Domestic Sewage
Sewage collected from residential areas harbours
a considerable concentration of pathogens, which
are microorganisms responsible for causing various
diseases. These pathogens are primarily expelled
through human feces, making sewage from urban
environments particularly rich in potentially harm-
ful germs. If these pathogens were to leak into natu-
ral water bodies, such as lakes or rivers, they could
directly threaten public health Cui et al., 2019.
Another significant issue associated with sewage
is the presence of organic chemicals that can decom-
pose over time. These biodegradable substances
are naturally found in sewage and are broken down
by bacteria and other microorganisms present in

The discharge of untreated sewage into lakes and
streams poses a significant risk to aquatic ecosystems.
Fish and other aquatic organisms depend significantly
on sufficient oxygen levels in the water for their sur-

disrupt this balance by reducing oxygen availability,
thereby endangering the health and sustainability of

In summary, sewage from households not only
contains a variety of pathogens that can threaten
human health if released into water bodies, but it
also introduces organic compounds that degrade
water quality by depleting oxygen levels essential for
aquatic organisms. Proper management and treatment
of sewage are crucial to mitigate these environmental
and public health risks associated with urban waste-
water disposal.
3.1.2	Solid Waste and Sediments
Improper disposal of solid waste poses a significant
threat to water quality by contributing substantially to
contamination. This issue encompasses a wide array
of solid waste types originating from various sources
such as private households, commercial enterprises,
institutions, and industrial facilities. Among these
wastes are garbage, rubbish, electronic waste, general
trash, sediment washed off from surfaces by rainwa-
ter (surface runoff), and debris from construction and
demolition activities Almusawi et al., 2022.
In some regions, the deliberate discharge of solid
waste into water bodies occurs due to several factors.
These include the lack of adequate infrastructure for
effective waste management, limited resources allo-
cated to waste disposal, and insufficient or poorly
enforced legislative measures. This problem is par-
ticularly acute in economically disadvantaged nations
where the capacity to manage solid waste is often
overwhelmed Jerin et al., 2022.
The consequences of such practices are far-reach-
ing. They disrupt the delicate ecological balance of
aquatic ecosystems by reducing the penetration of
sunlight into the water, which is essential for photo-
synthesis and the sustenance of aquatic life Blanchet
et al., 2022. Moreover, when solid waste settles and
accumulates on the seabed or riverbed, it can smother
and suffocate bottom-dwelling species, exacerbating
ecological harm Brule et al., 2022.
Addressing the issue of improper solid waste
disposal requires concerted efforts in developing
comprehensive waste management infrastructure,
allocating sufficient resources for waste disposal
and recycling programs, and enforcing stringent

3.1.3	Toxic waste
Toxic waste comprises a wide range of substances
that present considerable dangers to both the envi-
ronment and human health. These substances span
various categories, each with its own distinct hazards:
they can be poisonous, emitting toxins that harm liv-
ing organisms; radioactive, emitting harmful radia-
tion; explosive, posing immediate danger if ignited;
teratogenic, capable of causing birth defects in devel-
oping fetuses; carcinogenic, potentially leading to
cancer development; mutagenic, capable of damag-
ing genetic material; and bio-accumulative, building
up within organisms as they consume contaminated
prey, ultimately concentrating in higher levels of the
food chain. Each of these characteristics underscores
the complex and multifaceted risks associated with
toxic waste, necessitating rigorous management and
containment practices to safeguard ecosystems and
public health Jaishankar et al., 2014.
Examples of sources for these hazardous chemicals
are widespread. They include pesticides like chlor-
dane, dieldrin, and heptachlor, which are carried into
water bodies through surface runoff from agricultural
fields and residential lawns. Additionally, industrial
plants and chemical processing facilities discharge
wastewater containing toxic metals such as lead, mer-
cury, and chromium, contributing further to environ-
mental contamination Lalah et al., 2022.
Land pollution, another significant concern, intro-
duces debris and rubble into water systems through
various means like wind, animal transport, or pre-
cipitation. This process directly contaminates water
bodies, and over time, pollutants can accumulate
and eventually reach the oceans from inland water-
ways Shi et al., 2024. This pollution not only affects
the aesthetic quality of aquatic environments but also
poses immediate threats to specific species and entire
ecosystems Zhang et al., 2024.
Furthermore, specific categories of solid waste,
such as plastics and electronic waste, are acknowl-
edged for their potential to degrade and emit harmful
chemicals into the environment during decomposi-
tion. These materials are categorized as hazardous or
toxic waste due to their ability to release substances
that can adversely affect ecosystems and human
health over time. Proper management and disposal
practices are essential to mitigate these environmen-
tal risks associated with the breakdown of such waste
materials Abdel-Shafy & Mansour, 2018. This under-
standing underscores the critical need for proper dis-
posal methods such as landfills, which are designed
to contain and mitigate the risks associated with such
waste materials.
3.1.4	Pathogens
Bacteria, viruses, protozoans, and helminths repre-
sent the primary types of organisms recognized for
their potential to endanger human health through
waterborne transmission. In the realm of water qual-
ity research, indicator organisms play a pivotal role
due to the considerable difficulty and expense asso-
ciated with detecting pathogenic organisms in water
samples, which often contain low concentrations
of these hazards. These bacterial markers are exten-
sively utilized for their reliability in identifying the
presence of fecal matter, which can harbor harmful
microorganisms Motlagh & Yang, 2019.
Waterborne microorganisms have the potential to
cause a range of illnesses in both humans and ani-
mals. Pathogens have been sporadically detected
in surface waters contaminated by sewage or other
sources. These pathogens have been associated with
a variety of health problems in people, highlighting
the critical importance of monitoring and maintaining
water quality standards. Additionally, Schistosoma-
type parasitic worms have been implicated in certain
instances of waterborne diseases, emphasizing the
diverse nature of health risks associated with contam-
inated water sources Richiardi et al., 2023.
3.1.5	Inorganic compounds
Inorganic water contamination encompasses several
significant sources. One notable example is the pres-
ence of ammonia, which often originates from the
waste products of food processing industries. These
industries release ammonia as a byproduct of their
operations, contributing to water pollution. Another
concern is the introduction of heavy metals into urban
storm drains by vehicles. These metals, such as lead
and mercury, accumulate from automotive emissions

entering water bodies. Furthermore, the application
of acid mine drainage fertilizers can lead to the gen-

Yang et al., 2024a. These chemicals, essential for

Additionally, sediment, known as silt, can be found
in runoff from various human activities. These activi-

projects, sewage systems, logging operations, and
land clearance activities Adams et al., 2016. Sedi-
ment runoff poses challenges by clouding water and
affecting aquatic habitats.
Freshwater salinization, caused by saline runoff, is
another critical issue affecting freshwater ecosystems.
This phenomenon refers to the pollution of freshwater
bodies due to the introduction of salts from various
sources. A significant contributor to salinity in fresh-
water systems is secondary salinization, also referred
to as anthropogenic salinization, stemming largely
from the extensive application of de-icing salts on
roads. This phenomenon occurs when salts used to
melt ice and snow on roadways eventually leach into
surrounding soil and water bodies. The process is
exacerbated by urbanization and infrastructure devel-
opment, which increase the frequency and volume of
salt applications. As a result, freshwater ecosystems
experience elevated salinity levels, impacting aquatic
life, water quality, and ecosystem health Cunillera-
Montcusi et al., 2022. These salts enter water bodies
through runoff and can alter the chemical composi-
tion of freshwater ecosystems, impacting aquatic life
and water quality. For further insights into the com-
plexities of nutrient pollution and its implications for
water quality, please refer to the dedicated section on
this topic.
3.2	Social Impact
The impact of human activities and our interactions
with the natural environment have placed Earth's eco-
systems in significant peril. Our actions, from indus-
trialization to urbanization, agriculture, and resource
extraction, have collectively threatened the delicate
balance of ecological systems worldwide. These
activities have resulted in habitat destruction, pol-
lution, climate change, and the depletion of natural
resources, all of which have profound implications for
biodiversity and the stability of ecosystems. Address-
ing these challenges requires concerted efforts in
conservation, sustainable development, and global
cooperation to ensure the resilience and health of our
planet's diverse ecosystems for future generations.
This peril stems largely from the disregard of tradi-
tional regulations and ethical considerations, resulting
in widespread depletion and degradation of natural
resources. This includes the deterioration of water
quality and air purity, which are essential for sustain-
ing life on our planet Gour et al., 2024. The pursuit of
economic growth and an improved standard of living
has driven these destructive practices, often without
due consideration for their long-term consequences.
The evolution from a "sociology of environment"
to "environmental sociology" reflects a broader aca-
demic and societal shift. It emphasizes the complex
relationships between humans and their environment,
aiming to guide human activities in ways that pro-
mote ecological health and sustainability Mihalache
et al., 2024. This paradigmatic shift underscores the
need for integrated approaches that consider environ-
mental, social, and economic factors to ensure a bal-
anced and resilient ecosystem.
Central to maintaining ecological balance is the
diversity of organisms within ecosystems. From
microscopic bacteria to large marine mammals, each
species plays a crucial role in the intricate web of life.
The interactions within these ecosystems are delicate
and interdependent; any disturbance can have cas-
cading effects throughout the entire ecosystem Wei-
land-Brauer, 2021. For instance, the introduction of
excessive chemicals into water bodies can fuel algal
blooms, leading to oxygen depletion and the crea-
tion of "dead zones" where marine life cannot survive
Iruoghene, 2022, Faquih et al., 2024. Such phenom-
ena not only threaten aquatic biodiversity but also
disrupt fisheries and other human activities dependent
on healthy marine ecosystems.
Moreover, toxic algal blooms pose additional
risks, releasing harmful neurotoxins that can affect
marine mammals like whales and sea turtles, as well
as human health in coastal communities. Industrial
and municipal wastewater further exacerbates these
issues, introducing pollutants such as heavy met-
als and chemicals into freshwater systems Dorantes-
Aranda, 2023. These pollutants accumulate in
organisms, reducing their lifespan and reproductive
capacities, thereby endangering entire populations

and ecosystems Pasaoglulari Aydinlik et al., 2021.
Predators consuming contaminated prey, such as
larger fish accumulating mercury from smaller fish,
exemplify how human activities can lead to hazard-
ous levels of pollutants in the environment Ritger
et al., 2018. These pollutants not only affect marine
life but also pose risks to human health through the
consumption of contaminated seafood. Beyond
chemical pollutants, marine ecosystems face threats
from physical pollutants like marine debris. Plastics
and other waste materials can entangle, suffocate,
or be ingested by marine organisms, leading to star-
vation, injury, or death Saravanan et al., 2024. This
underscores the urgent need for global efforts to
reduce plastic waste and other marine debris through
improved waste management practices and interna-
tional cooperation. The challenges facing our ecosys-
tems are multifaceted and interconnected. Addressing
these challenges requires a holistic approach that inte-
grates scientific knowledge, policy interventions, and
public awareness. By promoting sustainable practices
and environmental stewardship, we can mitigate the
impacts of anthropogenic activities and safeguard the
biodiversity and ecological integrity of our planet for
future generations.
A considerable volume of solid waste, compris-
ing items such as plastic bags and soda cans, often
enters the marine environment through storm drains
and sewer systems, eventually making its way into
the oceans. The consequences for marine life have
been severe, affecting more than 200 different spe-
cies. Abandoned fishing equipment and various other
forms of waste Mahajan-Tatpate et al., 2024 exacer-
bates this impact.
Besides the accumulation of physical waste,
another critical concern is the rising acidity levels in
our oceans. This increase in acidity primarily stems
from the absorption of around a quarter of the annual
carbon emissions generated by burning fossil fuels.
The repercussions of this phenomenon are extensive:
it can interfere with the neurological processes of
marine life and impede the growth of shells in vari-
ous species, particularly shellfish. This acidification
poses a serious threat to marine ecosystems, affect-
ing their biodiversity and ecological balance Falken-
berg et al., 2020.
The phrase "water pollution" covers a diverse
array of pollutants that affect aquatic environments.
These contaminants include various chemicals,
pathogens such as bacteria and viruses, and physical
changes such as temperature fluctuations. Each type
of pollutant can have distinct effects on water qual-
ity and ecosystem health, highlighting the complex
and multifaceted nature of water pollution. Under-
standing and addressing these different sources of
contamination are essential for safeguarding water
resources and maintaining the ecological balance
of aquatic ecosystems Nguyen et al., 2024, Saxena
et al., 2022b. While many chemicals exist naturally
in water at controlled levels, elevated concentra-
tions of these natural substances can disrupt aquatic
ecosystems by blurring the distinction between
natural components and pollutants. Moreover, sub-
stances that deplete oxygen levels in water, originat-
ing from both human activities and natural sources
like decaying plant matter, can further stress aquatic
environments Gavrilescu, 2021. Turbidity reduces
light penetration, potentially hampering the growth
of aquatic plants and affecting the respiratory sys-
tems of certain fish species Syeed et al., 2023. This
phenomenon underscores the interconnectedness of

3.3.10	Nitrates
Nitrates are a class of chemical compounds that are
created through a specific chemical process in which
nitrogen atoms combine with oxygen atoms or ozone
molecules. This reaction typically involves the oxida-
tion of nitrogen, resulting in the formation of nitrate
ions (NO3—).
In nature, this process often occurs through a
series of steps in the nitrogen cycle. Initially, nitrogen
in the atmosphere, primarily in the form of nitrogen
gas (N2), is converted into more reactive forms such
as nitrogen oxides (NO and NO2) through processes
like lightning or microbial activity in the soil. These
nitrogen oxides then react with oxygen or ozone (O3)
in the atmosphere, especially under the influence of
sunlight and other environmental conditions, lead-
ing to the formation of nitrates Ng et al., 2017. These
compounds naturally exist in the environment and
are frequently present in a variety of foods and food
preservatives. Although they are found in nature, the
predominant source of nitrates is the commercial fer-
tilizers that are widely applied in modern agricultural
practices. These fertilizers are extensively used to
enhance crop yields and are a major contributor to the
levels of nitrates we encounter in the food supply and
the environment Singh & Saxena, 2021.
The European Union has established regula-
tions to control the levels of nitrates and nitrites
in drinking water, setting maximum allowable
concentrations for nitrites Zendehbad et al., 2022.
These limits are crucial to ensure public health
and safety. When the concentrations of these com-
pounds exceed the specified limits, it is essential to
take immediate corrective actions to reduce them
to acceptable levels. Such actions may include
improving water treatment processes or reduc-
ing the use of nitrate-rich fertilizers in agriculture.
Failure to adhere to these regulations can result in
significant consequences for producers, including
financial penalties Ward et al., 2018.

3.4 Climate change
To comprehensively understand climate change, it
is essential to start by defining the term "climate."
Climate can be described in two primary ways. This
statistical description includes parameters such as
temperature, precipitation, wind, and other related
factors. Second, "climate" is often understood as the
average weather conditions observed in a specific
region over a long period. In essence, climate can be
generally defined as the state of the climatic system,
incorporating both the variability and averages of var-
ious atmospheric elements Koutsoyiannis, 2021.
Climate change, often referred to as global warm-
ing, encompasses the sustained elevation in Earth's
average surface temperature over an extended period.
This phenomenon primarily stems from human
activities, notably the combustion of fossil fuels such
as coal, oil, and natural gas. When these fuels are
burned, they emit GHGs like carbon dioxide (CO2)
into the atmosphere. GHGs possess the capability to
trap heat, contributing to the warming of the Earth's
surface. They include significant environmental
impacts such as heightened sea levels due to melt-
ing polar ice caps and glaciers. Moreover, climate
change exacerbates the frequency and intensity of
extreme weather events such as hurricanes, droughts,
and floods. These consequences not only pose threats
to ecosystems and biodiversity but also have pro-
found socio-economic implications, affecting agricul-
ture, water resources, and human health worldwide.
Addressing climate change involves global efforts to
reduce GHG emissions, adapt to its impacts, and fos-
ter sustainable practices to mitigate future risks to the
planet and its inhabitants Saxena et al., 2023, Mani-
kandan & Saraswathi, 2023, Wei et al., 2020.
A strong consensus exists among scientists world-
wide that climate change is a real and urgent issue.
Major scientific organizations, such as the National
Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA), the
National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration
(NOAA), and the Environmental Protection Agency
(EPA) of the United States, have confirmed that
human activities are the primary drivers of recent cli-
mate change. These organizations highlight the pro-
found impacts of climate change on natural systems,
human health, and global economies.
The Geneva Report published by the World Health
Organization (WHO) provides a detailed analysis
of how global climate change measures affect pub-
lic health. Figure 3 from this report illustrates the

respiratory disorders due to poor air quality, and the
spread of vector-borne diseases.
Despite various mitigation efforts, the Earth's cli-
mate system continues to experience significant stress
and pressures. The ongoing emission of GHG and
other pollutants exacerbates the challenges posed by
climate change. As a result, it is crucial to implement
effective strategies for reducing GHG emissions,
enhancing resilience to climate impacts, and promot-

4.1	Structure of Atmospheric Pollution
Climate change and air pollution are significant issues
that pose serious threats to the global environment
and human health. The complex interplay between
these two challenges highlights their collective impact
on our planet. Urban areas are especially burdened
by high levels of major air pollutants, including sul-
fur dioxide, nitrogen dioxide, and carbon monoxide.
These harmful substances primarily originate from
the burning of fossil fuels such as fuel oil, gasoline,
and natural gas. This combustion process takes place
in power plants, vehicles, and a variety of industrial
activities. The high concentration of these pollutants
in cities contributes to deteriorating air quality, which
in turn exacerbates health problems and environmen-
tal degradation Roberts, 2021.
Ozone, a reactive gas and a major component of
smog, forms in the atmosphere through intricate
chemical reactions involving nitrogen dioxide and
volatile organic compounds, including those emit-

colourless, poses a substantial risk to human life by
interfering with the bloodstream's oxygen-carrying
capacity. In high concentrations, carbon monoxide

leading to fatalities.
The combustion processes responsible for emit-
ting these pollutants also release other hazardous
substances into the surrounding environment. When

cant threats to sensitive ecosystems such as lakes and
forests. The detrimental effects of acid rain extend
to endangering aquatic life, harming vegetation, and
altering soil chemistry, thereby impacting entire
landscapes.

emissions from fossil fuel combustion, the formation
of ozone and other pollutants, and their culmination
in acid rain illustrate the interconnected challenges
of climate change and air pollution. Addressing these

ogy, and global cooperation to mitigate their far-

4.1.1	Ozone (O3) Impact in the Atmosphere
Ozone (O3), a molecule composed of three oxy-
gen atoms, exists in varying concentrations near the
Earth's surface as well as in the upper atmosphere.
When found at ground level, particularly on hot and
sunny days, ozone becomes a significant concern for
human health. It forms a crucial part of smog, a com-
plex mixture of pollutants, and is officially classified
as a hazardous air pollutant due to its potential adverse
impacts on human health and the environment.
Exposure to elevated levels of ground-level
ozone can lead to a range of health problems. These
include respiratory issues such as aggravated asthma,
decreased lung function, and increased susceptibility
to respiratory infections. Additionally, ozone expo-
sure can exacerbate existing cardiovascular condi-
tions and cause inflammation of the airways, leading
to discomfort and reduced quality of life. The severity
of these health effects depends on factors such as the
duration and intensity of ozone exposure, as well as
individual susceptibility Bradu et al., 2022.
Particles smaller than 10 ^m can reach the lungs
and potentially enter the bloodstream, causing irrita-
tion and inflammation of the airways. This can exac-
erbate respiratory conditions, and may increase the
risk of respiratory infections Edo et al., 2024a.
Therefore, both ground-level ozone and particu-
late matter pose significant health risks, especially to
vulnerable individuals with pre-existing respiratory
conditions. Understanding these pollutants and their
effects is crucial for implementing effective air qual-
ity management strategies to protect public health and
the environment Upadhyay, 2020.

Carbon monoxide (CO) is a dangerous gas that is both
colorless and odorless, making it undetectable by
human senses. It is produced when carbon-containing
fuels like natural gas, gasoline, and wood undergo
incomplete combustion. This can happen in various
settings, including vehicles, power plants, wildfires,
and incinerators, leading to the release of CO into
the atmosphere. Vehicles, particularly in urban areas,
are significant contributors to outdoor CO emissions.
Traffic congestion exacerbates this issue, causing high
levels of CO pollution. Power plants that burn fossil
fuels and wildfires also add to CO emissions. Incin-
erators used for waste disposal can emit CO when the
combustion process is incomplete. The presence of
CO in the atmosphere is a major environmental and
public health concern. Efforts to reduce CO emissions
focus on improving combustion efficiency, regulating
industrial processes, and reducing traffic congestion
to mitigate the harmful effects of this invisible gas
Sheoran et al., 2022.
In addition to direct sources of combustion, other
atmospheric compounds, including methane, non-
methane hydrocarbons, and various organic mol-

4.1.4	Nitrogen Oxides
Nitrogen dioxide (NO2) is not commonly released
into the atmosphere in its primary form. Instead, it
is produced through chemical interactions involv-
ing nitrogen oxide (NO) and various other airborne
compounds. A significant portion of NO2 in the
environment originates from human activities, par-
ticularly the burning of fossil fuels such as coal, oil,
and gasoline, with vehicular emissions being a nota-
ble contributor Sharma et al., 2024 and Saxena et al.,
2024d. In addition to transportation, several industrial
processes are responsible for NO2 emissions. These
processes include the production of nitric acid, which
is extensively used in fertilizers and explosives, as
well as welding activities, metal refining, and oil
refining operations. Other sources include commer-
cial manufacturing practices and food processing
activities Lalah et al., 2022. Through these processes,
industries significantly contribute to the overall levels
of nitrogen dioxide in the atmosphere, impacting air
quality and public health.
Natural sources like bacteria and volcanic activity
also release nitrogen oxides into the environment in
significant quantities. The control of combustion pro-
cesses plays a crucial role in managing NOx emissions.
Strategies involve adjusting combustion parameters
to lower peak temperatures, reducing the time spent
at high temperatures, and optimizing oxygen levels in
combustion zones Abdel-Shafy & Mansour, 2018. It's
noteworthy that NOx production increases exponen-
tially at temperatures exceeding 1200 degrees Celsius.
Nitrogen dioxide primarily impacts human health
through its effects on the respiratory system. Children
are particularly vulnerable, with exposure to NO2
linked to increased susceptibility to respiratory infec-
tions and potential long-term impacts on lung func-
tion. Elevated NO2 levels in ambient air are associ-
ated with higher rates of hospitalizations and deaths
related to respiratory disorders. Moreover, NO2 can
compromise the lungs' natural defenses against bacte-
ria, potentially worsening asthma symptoms and res-
piratory conditions Cunillera-Montcusi et al., 2022.

4.1.5	Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbons
Carbohydrates, crude oil, and petrol are examples of
compounds that include polycyclic aromatic hydro-
carbons (PAHs), a group of chemicals characterized
by multiple fused aromatic rings. PAHs are pro-
duced during the incomplete combustion of organic
materials such as coal, tobacco, oil, and gas. This
process can lead to the direct formation of PAHs
or their attachment to airborne particles, which can

high-temperature cooking methods, like grilling or

Naphthalene, a specific synthetic PAH, is com-

is another major source of PAHs, releasing numer-
ous types into the air, which can pose health risks to
smokers and those exposed to second-hand smoke.
Therefore, PAHs are prevalent in many everyday sce-
narios, from industrial activities to household prac-
tices, highlighting the widespread nature of these
potentially hazardous compounds.
In rural regions, the major contributors to PAH
pollution stem from domestic and agricultural activi-
ties. These include practices like burning crop resi-
dues, using solid fuels for cooking and heating, and
applying chemical treatments to crops. Conversely,
in urban environments, PAH contamination primar-
ily originates from industrial processes, vehicular
emissions, and various residential sources. The dense
network of transportation, extensive industrial opera-
tions, and routine domestic activities like cooking and
heating significantly add to the urban PAH load.
PAH concentrations exhibit notable seasonal
fluctuations, reaching their peak during the win-
ter months. This is followed by a gradual decrease
through spring and autumn, with the lowest levels
observed in summer. Several factors contribute to this
seasonal pattern. During winter, the incomplete com-
bustion of fossil fuels is more prevalent due to height-
ened heating needs, which leads to higher emissions
of PAHs. Additionally, the colder temperatures and
reduced sunlight in winter hinder the photodegra-
dation of PAHs, causing these pollutants to persist
longer in the environment. Poor atmospheric diffusion
conditions, such as calm winds and low temperatures,
further exacerbate the accumulation of PAHs during
the colder months, as these meteorological conditions
limit the dispersion and dilution of airborne pollut-
ants Georgaki et al., 2023.
PAHs tend to accumulate in soil and sediment,
which can result in the contamination of groundwater,
plants, and the food chain. When PAHs are present in
the environment, they can seep into the soil and per-
sist for long periods due to their chemical stability.
This persistence leads to significant environmental
contamination. For example, PAHs in contaminated
soil can be taken up by plant roots and then trans-
ported to other parts of the plant, including leaves,
stems, and fruits. This process means that PAHs can
enter the food chain when humans and animals con-
sume these plants. The widespread presence of PAHs
in the environment makes exposure to these harm-
ful compounds almost unavoidable. Individuals can
be exposed to PAHs through three main pathways:
inhalation, ingestion, and skin contact. Inhalation can
occur when PAHs are present in the air, particularly
in areas with significant industrial emissions or where
combustion processes, such as vehicle exhaust or
tobacco smoke, release these compounds. Ingestion
happens when PAHs enter the body through consum-
ing contaminated food or water. Finally, skin contact
with contaminated soil or water can also lead to PAH
absorption Steinmaus, 2016.
To minimize cancer risk, it is crucial to consider a
no-effect threshold when assessing the health risks of

PAHs. This threshold helps determine safe exposure
levels, ensuring public health protection by identify-

In aquatic and marine environments, PAHs can
have detrimental effects on cardiovascular health,
impacting all life stages of fish. The heart, being a
vital organ, plays a critical role in extracting oxygen
from the environment and delivering it to the mito-
chondria within cells. Damage to the heart caused by
PAH exposure can therefore have far-reaching conse-
quences for aquatic life Schwartz et al., 2021.
Furthermore, PAHs are not limited to environmen-
tal pollution but are also found in various categories
of food and beverages. This widespread presence
makes PAHs a significant concern for both environ-
mental health and food safety Seo et al., 2023. The
comprehensive study of PAHs and their impacts
remains crucial to developing effective guidelines and
protective measures against their harmful effects.
PAHs represent a complex compound consisting
of both lighter and heavier molecules. Among these,
BaA, Chr, BbF, and BaP are identified as the princi-
pal PAHs found in foods according to the European
Commission Omarova et al., 2018. These compounds
can contaminate food through various pathways,
including production processes and environmental
pollution.
PAHs originate from multiple sources such as the
production and utilization of coal, oil, and petro-
leum products, as well as the combustion of wood
and other plant materials. Soil contamination can
occur when these substances are burned, leading to
PAHs being present in agricultural lands where food
is grown Wang & Qian, 2021. Furthermore, inten-
tional industrial processes like the cracking of com-
plex hydrocarbons in petroleum can produce what
are known as pyrogenic PAHs. Urban areas, due to
higher concentrations of industrial activities, often
experience elevated levels of these PAHs Koutsoyian-
nis, 2021.
The impact of PAHs extends beyond their chemi-
cal structure. They are known to absorb visible and
UVA light and can generate reactive species and
intermediates. These reactive compounds have the
potential to cause harm to biological structures such
as cell membranes, nucleic acids (DNA and RNA),
and proteins Skendzic et al., 2021.

risks associated with these ubiquitous environmental

4.2 Air Pollution and Human Health
Exposure to elevated levels of air pollution poses
numerous adverse consequences for physical health.
Individuals exposed to such pollution face increased
risks of developing respiratory infections, heart prob-
lems, and lung cancer Sheoran et al., 2022. These
health impacts can manifest both in the short term,
causing immediate discomfort and illness, and in the
long term, contributing to chronic conditions and
reduced life expectancy. Moreover, individuals who
already suffer from pre-existing health conditions are
particularly susceptible to exacerbated symptoms and
complications due to air pollution.
Children, whose respiratory systems are still devel-
oping, are at risk of long-term respiratory issues.
The elderly, whose immune systems may be com-
promised, are more likely to experience severe health
consequences from pollution exposure. Addition-
ally, socioeconomically disadvantaged populations,
including those with limited access to healthcare and
living in polluted urban areas, face heightened risks
Sheoran et al., 2022.
Among the pollutants, PM2.5 particles are espe-
cially harmful to human health. These fine particles
are small enough to penetrate deep into the lungs,
leading to respiratory and cardiovascular problems.

Maciejczyk et al., 2021. The variability in health

status, and lifestyle choices.
Factors such as residential proximity to industrial
facilities, traffic congestion, and inadequate environ-
mental regulations contribute to higher pollution lev-
els in these communities Robb & Techtmann, 1981.
Consequently, these populations are more likely to
suffer from the negative health effects associated with
air pollution, perpetuating health disparities Sellami

Regular exposure to wood smoke presents another
significant health concern. Wood smoke contains a
complex mixture of pollutants that pose significant
risks to respiratory health. Among these pollutants
are fine particles, which can penetrate deep into the
lungs and cause various respiratory issues. VOCs are
also present in wood smoke, and they contribute to
air pollution and can irritate the respiratory system.
Additionally, wood smoke contains CO, a toxic gas
that can interfere with the oxygen-carrying capacity
of blood, leading to potential respiratory and cardio-
vascular problems. Dioxins, another component of
wood smoke, are highly toxic compounds that can
have long-term health effects, including respiratory
issues and an increased risk of cancer. The combi-
nation of these pollutants in wood smoke can have
severe adverse effects on respiratory health, making it
a significant environmental and public health concern
Marques et al., 2021. This underscores the impor-
tance of addressing not only industrial and vehicular
sources of air pollution but also household sources
like wood burning to mitigate health risks.
Wood smoke, beyond just containing soot, gases,
and wood tars, encompasses a diverse array of other
substances. The short-term exposure to fine particu-
late matter present in the air due to wood smoke can
notably exacerbate existing lung conditions, provoke
asthma attacks, and lead to episodes of acute bron-
chitis. Furthermore, this exposure can potentially
elevate the risk of respiratory infections, posing sig-
nificant health risks to those affected. Research has
also identified a link between brief exposures to these
particles and occurrences of irregular heartbeats and
heart attacks during clinical trials Ishika (2023). Fur-
thermore, these fine particles are associated with the
development of several serious diseases, including
COPD, chronic bronchitis, cardiovascular disease,
and there is even evidence suggesting a possible link
to lung cancer.
Ozone, normally beneficial high in the atmosphere
where it protects us from harmful ultraviolet radiation,
becomes problematic at ground level as a component
of smog. This ground-level ozone forms when oxygen
oxides and volatile organic compounds, both products
of fossil fuel combustion, react in the presence of sun-
light Zhang et al., 2023. Exposure to ozone, even for a
short duration, can result in a variety of acute symp-
toms including chest pain, persistent coughing, and
throat irritation. These immediate health effects can
be quite discomforting and indicative of the body's
adverse reaction to the pollutant. When exposure
extends over a longer period, the health implications
become more serious and enduring. Long-term expo-
sure to ozone is associated with the gradual deterio-
ration of lung function. This decline can manifest in
reduced breathing capacity and overall respiratory
efficiency. Furthermore, there is a significant cor-
relation between prolonged ozone exposure and the
heightened risk of developing COPD.
In addition to these risks, continuous exposure to
ozone can exacerbate pre-existing lung conditions.
Individuals with respiratory ailments such as asthma,
bronchitis, and emphysema may experience a wors-
ening of their symptoms. Over time, the severity of
these conditions can increase, leading to more fre-
quent and intense episodes of respiratory distress.
This cumulative effect highlights the insidious nature
of ozone as a persistent environmental hazard, capa-
ble of inflicting long-lasting damage to respiratory
health Gautam et al., 2023.
The combustion of sulphur-containing fossil fuels
results in the emission of sulphur dioxide gas into the
atmosphere Naidu et al., 2021. This substance aggra-
vates asthma symptoms, irritates the eyes, and can
cause respiratory infections due to its negative impact
on the circulatory system. Furthermore, sulphur diox-
ide is an essential component of acid rain, which is
responsible for the devastation of ecosystems world-
wide due to sulphuric acid, a result of sulphur diox-
ide's reaction with atmospheric water.

4.3 Air Pollution's Effects on the Environment
Air pollution stands as a formidable challenge in con-
temporary society Siddiqua et al., 2022. Its impact
extends across multiple dimensions, contributing
significantly to climate change and profoundly affect-
ing public and individual health. The consequences
are stark, with elevated rates of disease and mortal-
ity directly linked to exposure to pollutants Srivastava
et al., 2023. Furthermore, climate change exacerbates
these health risks by prolonging pollen seasons and
fostering conditions favourable for the proliferation
of allergenic air pollutants such as mold and pollen,
driven by more frequent and intense weather events
and increased flooding Ewuzie et al., 2021.
The crucial impact of GHG, especially CO2
and CH4, is central to the climate change problem.
These gases are crucial in modifying the dynamics
of Earth's climate. The role of these GHG in driving
climate change underscores the urgency of addressing
emissions to mitigate their far-reaching consequences
Srivastava et al., 2024. Notably, in 2018, the United
States' GHG emissions predominantly comprised car-
bon dioxide (81%) and CH4 (10%) Srivastava & Roy,
2023.
Figure 8 illustrates how air pollution affects the
environment, including climate change, ecosystem
degradation, and health impacts on flora and fauna. It
emphasizes the interconnected nature of these effects,
underscoring the urgency of mitigation strategies.
Recent studies Srivastava & Roy, 2023 highlight
the significant environmental impacts of oil and gas
drilling, particularly in the context of methane emis-
sions. These pollutants not only alter the chemi-
cal composition of these environments but also lead
to the release of harmful metals and the depletion
of essential nutrients in freshwater and forest soils.
These changes have detrimental effects: low pH levels
in freshwater can lead to the death of fish populations,
while in forest soils, nutrient loss can result in forest
degradation.
In recent decades, efforts to reduce sulphur dioxide
emissions have been successful in mitigating acidifi-
cation issues. However, ground-level ozone remains
a significant threat to vegetation. Ozone, which pen-
etrates plant leaves, disrupts the process of photosyn-
thesis, thereby hindering plant growth. Widespread
changes to ecosystem structure, as well as decreases
in habitat quality and biodiversity, have been associ-
ated to elevated amounts of ozone in the ground. In
agricultural settings, ozone pollution can reduce crop
yields and impair forest growth.

In terrestrial ecosystems, nitrogen deposition from
the atmosphere can lead to eutrophication under cer-
tain conditions. When nitrogen deposition surpasses
critical loads, it can trigger dangerous levels of
eutrophication, affecting ecosystem health and bio-
diversity. The concept of "critical load" emphasizes
that exceeding certain pollution thresholds can dis-
rupt both terrestrial and aquatic ecosystems, altering
the composition and diversity of species. Conversely,
keeping pollution levels below these critical thresh-
olds allows ecosystems to absorb deposited pollutants
without significant disruption.
Particulate matter (PM) pollution, including PM10
and PM2.5, also poses serious health risks. Coarse
particles can affect the nasal and upper respiratory
systems, while fine particles, due to their smaller size,
can penetrate deeper into the lungs and even enter the
bloodstream. Also, research shows that kids whose
exposure to PM2.5 levels are higher may have prob-
lems with brain development.
Overall, understanding and mitigating the envi-
ronmental and health impacts of these pollutants are
crucial for sustainable development and public health
protection. Continuing research and effective policies
are essential to address these complex challenges and
minimize their detrimental effects on ecosystems and
human well-being.

5	Conclusion
The challenges posed by human-induced pollution
and climate change demand urgent, multifaceted
action. Below are the key takeaways and implications
for addressing these issues effectively:
• Human Contribution to Pollution:
Human activities are the primary drivers of
water and air pollution, contributing signifi-
cantly to climate change.
Solutions require collective responsibility and
comprehensive strategies across societal and
economic systems.
• Environmental Preservation and Restoration:
Immediate Action: Investments are needed to
rectify ecological damage caused by pollution.
Sustainable Practices: Adoption of emission-
reducing technologies, conservation of natural
resources, and biodiversity promotion are critical.
Restoration Initiatives: Beyond preventing
degradation, proactive restoration of habitats
ensures thriving ecosystems.
• Educational and Technological Advancements:
Public awareness campaigns can shift behaviors
like littering and deforestation.
Advanced technologies targeting industrial and
power plant emissions are crucial for mitigating
air pollution and aligning with global environ-
mental objectives.
•	Policy and Governance:
Governments must create evidence-based poli-
cies that address the broad impacts of pollution
on public health and ecosystems.
Local frameworks tailored to specific environ-
mental conditions can serve as models for inter-
national adoption.
•	Role of Research and Innovation:
Ongoing research is vital for discovering inno-
vative pollution mitigation strategies.
Integrating research findings into policy ensures
adaptive and forward-looking environmental
health strategies.
• Global Implications:
Efforts at local and regional levels collectively
contribute to global sustainability.
Coordinated international action, inspired by
successful local practices, is imperative for
managing climate change and pollution at scale.
